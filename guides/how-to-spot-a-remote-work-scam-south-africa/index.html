How to Tell a Remote-Work Scam From a Real Listing

2026-08-30

Most “work from home” listings aimed at South Africans are not jobs. They are recruitment fees, deposit schemes, or funnels into someone else’s downline. The cruel part is that real remote work also exists — paid in dollars or pounds into a South African account every day — so the advice “just avoid everything” costs honest people honest income.

This guide gives you a checklist you can apply to any listing in about five minutes: the red flags that almost always mean scam, the green flags that almost always mean real, and what to do when you cannot tell.

The five red flags

1. You pay to get the job. Any “registration fee”, “training kit”, “laptop deposit” or “clearance certificate” you must buy before starting is the scam. Real employers deduct lawful costs from pay you have already earned; they do not invoice you before day one.

Be careful with the distinction, though, because it is where honest platforms get mistaken for scams and scams get mistaken for platforms. A fee to start is the red flag. A cut of money you have already earned is ordinary. Upwork, listed in this directory, is free to join but sells “Connects” credits that you spend bidding for work — that is a real cost with no guaranteed return, and worth understanding before you spend. No platform in this directory charges a joining fee, and this site never charges you anything.

2. The pay is absurd for the work. “R8,500 per week for data entry, no experience” is bait. Compare it against what real listings state: across the 1127 roles on this board right now, 299 state an actual figure and 828 do not — and the ones that do cluster in ranges you can sanity-check. A number wildly above every comparable role is not an offer; it is a hook.

3. They contacted you, and urgency does the selling. A WhatsApp forward with “only 3 spots left, interview tomorrow” is marketing, not hiring. Real remote hiring has a paper trail: a company domain that is not a day old, a named interviewer, a process that survives you asking questions.

4. The company cannot be found outside the ad. Search the company name plus “reviews”. Look for a real website with named people. Check the domain age. Scam operations reuse the same script with new names; someone has usually posted the identical message before you.

5. Payment goes through a person. Salary arriving from someone’s personal bank account, or “we pay via gift cards or crypto only”, ends the conversation. Employers pay from business accounts through traceable rails.

The four green flags

1. The posting names where you may live, specifically. Real remote employers say who can apply: “remote — South Africa”, “open to applicants in EMEA”, or an eligible-countries field naming your country. Vague global ads attract thousands of unqualified applications; specific ones signal a real hiring process. Every listing on this site carries its eligibility evidence for exactly this reason.

2. The process has steps and takes time. Application, screening, interview, offer. Nobody legitimate hires you inside one WhatsApp conversation, and nobody legitimate needs you to start before you have read a contract.

3. The money maths is boring. Real postings state pay in normal ranges and periods — “$1,200–$1,800 per month”, “R180 per hour” — or honestly say “competitive salary”. Boring is good. Exciting is bait.

4. There is a real product behind the role. You can name what the company sells and who buys it. Your work will have a manager, a team, outputs somebody pays for. If you cannot explain what the business does, pause.

The two-minute verification routine

  • Search the company name with “reviews”, then again with “scam”.
  • Open their site. Named humans? Real address? A jobs page consistent with the ad?
  • Check the email domain matches the company — hr@companyname.com, not companyname.careers.hiring2026@gmail.com.
  • Search the exact ad text in quotation marks. Copy-paste farms repeat themselves.
  • Ask one direct question, such as which time zone you would work. A real recruiter answers; a scammer deflects or flatters.

Where South African jobseekers actually get caught

Not usually by fake ads — by fake platforms. The shape is a site that looks like a job board, “verifies” you for a fee, then vanishes. The defence is to use platforms with a public track record, and never to pay at the door.

Every platform reviewed on this site was checked against its own published material before being listed, including the negative findings. Prolific, for example, is listed here explicitly as not open to South African residents, in their own words. A directory that only ever says yes is selling you something.

If you were scammed

Report it to SAPS, which opens a case number. Tell your bank immediately if money moved — reversals are sometimes possible within hours, not days. Report the ad to the platform where it ran. Then warn people in the groups where you found it: the same operation often relists under a new name within weeks.
