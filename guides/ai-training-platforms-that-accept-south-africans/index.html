Which AI Training Platforms Actually Accept South Africans

2026-08-24

The problem with AI training job lists

Most “AI training jobs for Africans” articles are written by people who have never applied. They list platforms that quietly reject South African applicants at signup, quote pay rates that do not exist, and recommend routes that dead-end at a “country not supported” screen.

This list works differently. A platform appears below only if we can point at its own published words about who may join, and we quote those words rather than summarise them. Where a platform does not name South Africa, we say so instead of implying otherwise. Where it publishes no pay rate, we leave the number out rather than estimate one.

That leaves four platforms. One of them is here to tell you not to bother. Everything below was checked on 22–24 August 2026.

Micro1 — the clearest evidence of the four

A US AI data-research company that hires contractors worldwide as “experts” to train and evaluate AI models. You do not get hired into a job: you join the platform, sit an AI interview, and are matched to client projects across engineering, science, medicine, law and other domains. It is part-time by design — micro1 expects roughly 15–20 hours a week and says outright that you may keep your day job.

Can a South African join? Yes, and the evidence is unusually good. Their FAQ answers it directly — “Yes. We hire talent from around the world” — and then names the places it cannot hire from: Afghanistan, Belarus, China, Cuba, DR Congo, Hong Kong, Iran, Iraq, Libya, Macao, Myanmar, North Korea, Russia, Somalia, South Sudan, Sudan, Syria, Venezuela, Ukraine and Yemen. South Africa is not on that list. An exclusion list that leaves South Africa out is stronger evidence than any “remote, global” tag.

What it pays: not published. Micro1 advertises “competitive compensation for expert knowledge” and states no rate anywhere on its expert pages, so we do not quote one. What their FAQ does set out is the structure: hourly on time you track and the project team approves; pay-per-task, where an approved task pays your hourly rate multiplied by a “Project Payout Multiple” no matter how long it actually took; or a flat fee per approved task. Which one applies is written into your contract. Agree the rate before you sign it.

How the money reaches you: USD, twice a month, through Deel. Their FAQ: “Payments are processed in USD on a bi-monthly basis (twice per month). As part of the onboarding process, you will receive a contract from us via Deel, our payroll platform.” KYC runs through Deel and nothing is paid until it clears. You supply your own laptop.

AfterQuery — open worldwide, but it does not name us

A San Francisco applied research lab that pays professionals to turn their working knowledge into AI training data. The expert marketplace is a separate site from the corporate one — the work is at experts.afterquery.com, not afterquery.com.

Can a South African join? Step one of their own sign-up says “We welcome experts worldwide (availability of projects may vary by country)”, under the headline “Work From Anywhere in the World”. AfterQuery does not name South Africa anywhere, and we have found no South African testimonial. Treat it as a platform that is open worldwide rather than one recruiting here. Their own caveat is the honest part and worth reading twice: being welcome to join is not the same as projects being available in your country.

What it pays: published per role, which is rarer than it should be. Rates on their expert site on 22 August 2026: Medical MD Expert $120–$200/hr; Finance Expert (Undergraduate / Early Career) $50/hr; Content Writer and AI Evaluation Specialist $35–$50/hr; General Expert / Data Annotator $25–$60/hr; and a global content evaluation role at $15. The spread is wide because it tracks the expertise, not the hours — read the individual role.

How the money reaches you: per task or per project. Their FAQ promises “fast, reliable payouts with multiple payment options available” but names no rail anywhere on the site. Confirm how money would reach a South African account before you take work on.

Getting in: create an account, then an interview or an asynchronous video, then a short skills assessment. Credential checks are real, but they also say plainly: “No prior AI experience required.” The bar is your existing profession, not AI. One current role is openly titled Undergraduate / Early Career, which is unusual on these platforms.

Mercor — eligible by absence, and not an entry-level platform

A San Francisco marketplace that matches professionals to AI training and evaluation projects for AI labs. You apply once and are matched to projects rather than hired into a job.

Can a South African join? Mercor does not name South Africa, and we will not pretend otherwise. What it says is “work on your schedule from anywhere you choose”. What its own listings show is better evidence than the slogan: of 408 live roles we read on 22 August 2026, 263 carried no country restriction at all and are therefore open to a South African. Of the 145 that were restricted, 97 were United States only, and exactly one named South Africa among about 180 eligible countries. So you are eligible for most of the board by the absence of a restriction, not because Mercor invites South Africans. Check the eligible countries on the individual listing before you spend time on it.

What it pays: Mercor advertises an “average contracted rate $109/hr”. The live board is lower than that headline. Across the 392 hourly roles we read, the midpoint of the advertised range had a median of $80/hr; across the 254 hourly roles with no country restriction — the ones a South African can actually take — a median of $72/hr, ranging $14 to $250. US-restricted roles pay better, at a median of $84. Their $109 figure may reflect rates actually agreed rather than advertised; we quote what the listings say.

How the money reaches you: weekly. Their experts page says “Get paid weekly for contributing your expertise”. The rail itself is not published — no Deel, Wise, PayPal or wire is named on any page we could reach, and their terms and FAQ pages return 404. Ask how you will be paid into South Africa before you accept a contract.

Be realistic about the bar. The board runs to biostatisticians, pharmacologists, clinical regulatory specialists, lawyers and senior engineers. This is not a first-remote-job platform. You are assessed by their AI rather than interviewed — only 3 of the 408 roles we read had a scheduled interview attached — and 291 of them hid the client’s name.

Prolific — the one that says no

Prolific is recommended in almost every list like this one. It should not be, not for a South African signing up today, and knowing that before you spend an evening on it is the single most useful thing on this page.

South Africa is not on Prolific’s supported-country list. Their eligibility page names 48 countries and South Africa is not among them: “Prolific is open to participants from most Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries” — and South Africa is not an OECD member. Their waitlist page is blunter still: “You may also be added to the waitlist if you don’t meet Prolific’s prescreening criteria, or if you reside in a country not supported by Prolific, such as a non OECD country.”

Everyone now joins through that waitlist, and Prolific states it “isn’t a queue, so your position doesn’t change over time”. South Africans who joined years ago are still working and being paid — the restriction tightened later — but someone signing up from South Africa today should expect to wait rather than to start. Eligibility is judged on where you live, not your passport.

Platforms we could not verify

Plenty of other names get recommended for AI training work — Braintrust, Turing, Ethos, Vetto, RemoExperts, Clickworker and Toloka among them. They are absent from this list for one reason: we could not find a published statement from any of them about whether a South African may join. That is not an accusation. It means we looked, found nothing quotable, and will not tell you a platform accepts you on the strength of a “global remote” tag.

If you try one of them anyway, the sequence that saves the most time is: find their eligible-countries page before you build a profile, and stop if there is not one.

What to watch out for

  • “Remote” does not mean South Africa. Many global boards advertise “remote” roles that are US-only or EU-only. Always check the eligible-countries section before applying.
  • “Worldwide” is not the same as available. AfterQuery says it best in its own caveat: projects may or may not exist in your country even when the door is open.
  • Unpublished pay is normal, invented pay is not. Two of these four publish no rate at all. That is ordinary for project-based work — but never accept a rate you have not seen in writing, and never pay a fee to apply or to “unlock” a listing. Legitimate platforms do not charge applicants.
  • Ask how you get paid before you start. Only micro1 names an actual payment rail. On the others, establish how money reaches a South African account before you do the work, not after.
  • This list changes. Platform availability for South Africans shifts. Every figure above was checked on the date given, and we update this post when a platform changes policy.
