How Money From a Foreign Employer Actually Reaches a South African Account

Published 6 September 2026

Everyone names the platforms. Nobody explains the plumbing.

Search for how to get paid in dollars from South Africa and you will find lists of platforms, a few affiliate pages promising $1,500 a month for data entry, and a lot of job-board feeds. What almost none of them tell you is the part that decides whether the money arrives: which method the platform pays through, whether that method works properly from here, and how long it sits in transit.

This page is built from the 33 platforms, out of 38 we have researched, that publish something about how they pay. Where a platform states its method we quote it. Where it does not, we say so instead of guessing.

What the platforms actually name

  • PayPal — 13 platforms. The most commonly named method, and the one with the biggest South African catch. See the next section.
  • Payoneer — 13 platforms. Equally common, and generally the smoother of the two from here.
  • Wise — 8 platforms. Usually offered alongside another option rather than on its own.
  • Direct bank transfer or EFT — 7 platforms. Including the ones that pay into a South African account in rand.
  • Deel — 6 platforms. Worth separating from the others, because Deel is not a payment method. It is a contracting and payroll layer: the platform hires you through Deel, Deel handles the contract and the compliance, and money reaches you through whichever method you set up inside it. Micro1 puts it plainly in their own FAQ — “you will receive a contract from us via Deel, our payroll platform” — and adds that nothing is paid until their identity check clears.

Those counts move as we research more platforms. They are computed from the database on every build rather than typed, so what you are reading is the current state and not a snapshot from whenever this page was written.

The PayPal catch, which is specific to South Africa

PayPal is the single most-named method on this board, and it does not work here the way it works in the countries most of these platforms are based in. You cannot simply press withdraw and have the money land in your bank account.

Every PayPal withdrawal in South Africa runs through First National Bank. PayPal’s own South African help centre is direct about it: “You will need an FNB Online Banking profile.” The withdrawal happens inside FNB’s site rather than PayPal’s — log in at fnb.co.za, click Forex, then PayPal Services, then Withdraw from PayPal.

The good news is that you do not have to move your banking. FNB’s own page says the service “allows you to withdraw funds paid into your PayPal account to your FNB or any linked South African bank account”, and that you can create a free FNB Online Banking profile for the purpose without holding an FNB account. Capitec, Nedbank, Absa and Standard Bank customers all use this route.

Two things to plan around. FNB quotes a fee of “a maximum of 1.51%”, tapering with transaction size. And the two sources disagree on timing: FNB says a withdrawal “reflects instantly in an FNB bank account and takes up to 3 business days to reflect in a non-FNB bank account”, while PayPal’s article for the same process says funds arrive “within 3 to 5 business days”. Budget for the longer of the two.

This matters more than it looks, because several platforms pay through PayPal and nothing else. UserTesting answers the question on their own site as bluntly as it can be answered: “Is PayPal the only way to get paid? Yes.” Prolific is PayPal-only too, and its own record on this site carries the warning in plain sight, that PayPal works differently in South Africa from most of the countries Prolific serves. TranscribeMe collects your PayPal details at registration. Testbirds uses SEPA transfer for EU residents and PayPal for everyone else, South Africa included.

If you are signing up to any of those four, sort the FNB profile out first. Doing it after you have earned money is how a small balance sits untouched for a month.

The platforms that will not say

This is the part the listicles leave out. A number of well-known platforms advertise how fast they pay while never naming how.

  • Mercor advertises weekly pay and “daily payouts $4M+” across the platform, but no method is published anywhere we could reach, and their terms and FAQ pages return 404.
  • AfterQuery’s FAQ offers “fast, reliable payouts with multiple payment options available” without naming one of them.
  • Arc.dev confirms only the shape: freelancers are paid for time worked, a tax form is required before payout, and Arc is “not responsible for any additional fees or charges” your bank adds. Arc’s blog does compare freelance payment methods in general, which is market commentary and not a statement of what Arc itself uses.
  • Assist World employs you directly — “The candidate will be employed by Assist World and contracted to the client” — but names no method and no currency in any of the postings we hold.
  • Toptal pays in USD by a process it calls fully automated, without saying what that process is.

None of that makes them scams, and two of them are among the better-known names in this market. It does mean one question belongs in your first real conversation with any of them, before you invest hours in an assessment: how, in what currency, and on what schedule would I be paid into South Africa? A company that cannot answer that quickly is telling you something.

The one that pays nothing at all

Superprof deserves its own line, because the answer is not a payment method and most write-ups get it wrong. Their own South African page says it outright: “It is not possible to receive payment via the Superprof platform in South Africa.” The student pays you directly, by cash, EFT or whatever the two of you agree, and Superprof states it “is not involved in the payment process between tutors and students”.

The upside is real: no commission, so “you will receive 100% of the lesson fee the student pays”. The downside is equally real and much less advertised. Nothing holds the money in escrow, and Superprof will not chase a student who does not pay. Agree terms before the first lesson, not after.

Getting paid in rand is not a downgrade

Some platforms convert and pay a South African account directly. Globaltize takes USD from the client and pays the South African hire in rand into a local bank account. Way With Words is unusually specific: “Our transcribers are paid monthly directly into their South African Bank Account, irrespective of who their account is with.” Cherry Assistant quotes rand on the listing itself.

That route skips the FNB step, the conversion spread and the wait. Whether it leaves you better off depends on the rate, which is a separate argument — we make it in rands or dollars.

What about SARS?

Short version, and we are not tax advisers: South Africa taxes residents on worldwide income, so money from a foreign company is declarable regardless of which method carried it or which currency it arrived in. SARS states the principle plainly — “residents are, subject to certain exclusions, taxed on their worldwide income, irrespective of where their income was earned”.

The R1.25 million foreign employment exemption you will see quoted everywhere is very widely misread, and it probably does not apply to you. We set out what SARS actually publishes, and why, in the tax guide. Read that before you assume either way.

Before you accept anything

  1. Ask which method, in which currency, on what schedule. Get it in writing.
  2. If the answer is PayPal, set up your free FNB Online Banking profile before your first payday.
  3. Check the minimum payout. Prolific will not release below a combined balance of about six pounds or dollars, italki sets $30, and Testbirds sets a EUR 6 balance. A small first month can sit locked behind a threshold.
  4. Check when the clock starts. Several platforms pay a set number of days after approval rather than after you finish the work, which is a different date entirely. We have counted those.
  5. Never pay a fee to get set up for payment. No platform on this site charges you to be paid.

Common questions

Do I need an FNB account to use PayPal in South Africa?

No, but you do need a free FNB Online Banking profile. PayPal's own South African help centre says you will need one, and FNB's page says the service pays out to your FNB account or any linked South African bank account. You can bank with Capitec or Nedbank and still withdraw.

Which payment method do most platforms use?

Across the 33 researched platforms that publish how they pay, PayPal and Payoneer are named most often, on 13 and 13 platforms respectively. Wise appears on 8 and direct bank transfer on 7.

Can a platform refuse to tell me how it will pay me?

Several do. Their own pages advertise fast or weekly payouts without naming a single method. That is not automatically a warning sign, but it does mean the question belongs in your first conversation rather than after you have done the work.

Do I pay South African tax on money from a foreign company?

SARS taxes residents on worldwide income, so foreign earnings are declarable. The R1.25 million foreign employment exemption is widely misread: it applies to services rendered outside South Africa, not to work you do from home here. We are not tax advisers, so read SARS directly or speak to one.
